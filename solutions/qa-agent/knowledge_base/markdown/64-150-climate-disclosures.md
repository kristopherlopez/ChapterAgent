## **Long-term scenarios** 

This year we performed three long-term climate scenarios based on the NGFS scenarios. They explore futures with high transition risk (net zero 2050), higher transition risk (delayed transition) and higher physical risk (current policies). These three NGFS scenarios were selected to enable the assessment of a range of plausible climate physical and transition risk outcomes. To date, global progress against the goals of the Paris Agreement has lagged, increasing risk of the net zero 2050 scenario being out of reach. The delayed transition and current policies scenarios were selected as the Bank believes slower global progress in climate policy increases the probability of either scenario occurring. The NGFS scenario macroeconomic forecasts were tailored to the Australian context by an external economic advisory firm (Oxford Economics), and then modelled using the Bank’s suite of stress testing models to determine balance sheet and credit loss results extending out to 2050.

## **Outcomes of our long-term scenarios** 

Outcomes under all three long-term scenarios indicate the Bank’s business model remains resilient, and reinforces our approach to supporting Australia’s transition to net zero by 2050 and building climate resilience. While severe weather may cause substantial losses in some years in the long term, the financial impacts of climate-related risks are not currently considered material to the Bank. This is based on modelling which shows minimal impact on loan impairment expenses and average operating profit over time.

The introduction of strong climate policies, immediately under net zero 2050, and after 2030 under a delayed transition, are assumed to increase production costs and reduce demand for emissions-intensive industries such as coal and gas mining, manufacturing and livestock farming. This results in a drag on the economy and leads to moderate increases in unemployment, which are felt more immediately under the net zero 2050 scenario. However, the net zero 2050 future requires less aggressive government policies allowing the economy to more gradually transition away from emissions-intensive industries over a longer timeframe. 

The cash rate is assumed to remain low under these scenarios, supporting sustained growth in house prices. This limits average credit losses for the Bank due to the continued strong average loan-to-value ratio of borrowers. However, some industries and regions are more impacted than others under these higher transition risk scenarios, such as mining, utilities, manufacturing and agriculture, and regional areas exposed to coal and gas extraction industries. 

The net zero 2050 scenario has the better outcome for the Bank in the long term as the orderly nature of the transition results in higher growth and lower credit losses. The delayed transition scenario has slower growth between 2035 to 2045 due to the disorderly nature of the transition before recovering.

The purpose of these NGFS-aligned scenarios is to identify impact trends that climate change could have over time on the broader economy, certain industry sectors and ultimately the Bank. The scenarios do not attempt to predict the timing and severity of individual acute physical or abrupt transition events that could impact the Bank’s financial performance in any given year. Rather, the cumulative effects of such events are factored into macroeconomic variables used to model these trends. Although this approach enables a clearer view of any trends over time, it likely underestimates potential losses to the Bank because it does not account for localised declines, or direct impacts on, asset values from severe climate physical or transition events that could occur in any given year. Therefore, three acute, event-driven scenarios were conducted to supplement this view (see pages 152–153).
