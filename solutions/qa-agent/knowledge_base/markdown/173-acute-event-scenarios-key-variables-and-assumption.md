- **Insurance coverage (non-retail)** – Overall insurance rate assumptions were internally developed and allocated using random sampling. 

- **Insurability declines** – Following each cyclone, insurance premiums were increased by ~20% and take-up rates were reduced. 

- **Mortgage repayment stress** – The ability of borrowers to make mortgage repayments was stressed through factoring in the extent to which costs for demolition, repair and alternate property rental during repair are covered by insurance, and the repair period. Customers were assumed to have defaulted where the costs to repair and sell the property exceeded the equity in the property, or where the customer was in negative equity. 

- **Unemployment rates** – A significant rise in unemployment rates was assumed to occur in the cyclone years with an increase of 8% and 20% from 2024 by the end of year two for Brisbane and regional South-East QLD, respectively. Unemployment improves from year three as infrastructure is repaired and jobs are created during the rebuild. 

- **Residential building value declines** – Aligned to damage ratios applied to each property. A linear return to pre-cyclone values over the repair period is assumed. 

- The selection of the drought affected areas and the segmentation of these areas into lower and higher drought impact was based on data from both the Bank’s productivity mapping tool and climate stress index tool (PMT-CSI tool) and the Water Resources Institute, Aqueduct (Version 4) dataset, which identifies areas expected to suffer from high water stress in the future under their 2030 pessimistic scenario. 

**Modelling approach** 

- Macroeconomic and financial variables corresponding to the scenario were designed in collaboration with environmental risk teams and agribusiness credit risk experts. Property value parameters were adjusted based on their allocation to low or high impact regions. 

- The impact of macroeconomic and financial variables was assessed to arrive at final agriculture ANZSIC-level credit rating movements. These ANZSIC rating movements and relevant macroeconomic variables were subsequently processed through the Bank’s non-retail stress testing model to estimate expected credit losses for customers in both high-impact and low-impact regions. 

- For adjacent agriculture industries such as food manufacturing and wholesaling, ANZSIC rating movements were directly provided by agribusiness subject matter experts and applied to the non-retail stress test model to estimate expected credit losses.

   - **Property value impacts** – Properties in high-impact regions initially maintain their value due to government support and no action taken by banks. Deterioration in value commences towards the end of year four and continues until the cumulative reduction peaks at ~40% in year eight. The low-impact areas are assumed to increase in value by 10%. 

   - **International commodity prices** – Assumed to remain stable. 

   - **Government support** – Assumed in the form of a mix of income assistance, concessional loans, tax relief, and water and transport subsidies. However, after four years it is assumed to be insufficient to compensate for the combination of rising interest rates and declining revenue. 

- **Residential land value declines** – Varied by flood risk ratings for each property up to reductions of 35%. Land values were assumed to recover within a year in Brisbane, quicker than regional areas which were assumed to recover in five years. 

- **Insurance companies and re-insurers** – Assumed to remain solvent. 

- **Government support** – Assumed to be limited. 

- **Business disruption** – Infrastructure damage, including to roads and rail lines, interrupts supply chains causing local production to decline by: 

- 10% across non-impacted areas of QLD for three months after each cyclone. 

- – 30% in moderate and severe impacted zones for three months after each cyclone.
