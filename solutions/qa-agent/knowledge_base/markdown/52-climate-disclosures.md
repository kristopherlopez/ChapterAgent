## **Our sector goals[ 1]** 

## **Australian housing** 

**In-scope drawn lending as a proportion of Group TCE 41.7%** 

## **Sector overview and outlook** 

As at 30 June 2024, Australian housing accounted for 63.9% of our in-scope drawn lending. Residential buildings in Australia use approximately 24% of overall electricity and contribute over 10% of total carbon emissions.[ 2] Home emissions are mainly driven by electricity and gas for heating or cooling, cooking and hot water. While many households have made home energy upgrades, further work by industry and government is required to support homeowners in apartments and townhouses where energy efficiency measures are more difficult to adopt. 

## **Our goal and portfolio** 

As at 30 June 2024, the emissions intensity of our portfolio was 35.0 kgCO2-e/m[2] , a reduction of 3.8 kgCO2-e/m[2] compared to our 2021 baseline, 0.1 kgCO2-e/m[2] compared to June 2023 and is 17% above the reference scenario. The modest reduction compared to 2023 partly reflects a change in the data provider we use to calculate a home’s floorspace and number of bedrooms, and limitations in using publicly available data sets. Our financed emissions calculation draws on publicly available household energy consumption benchmarks meaning, at times, it is unable to account for the impact of increased solar and battery installations or changing energy usage by our customers.

## **Our intention and activities** 

While achieving our 2030 housing goal will be challenging, the Bank has no intention of reducing our lending to Australian housing customers to achieve it. We remain committed to supporting our customers on their path to home ownership or property investment, and where practicable, helping them reduce their energy bills and ease cost-of-living pressures. Our Home Energy Upgrades service aims to help bridge knowledge gaps and simplify decision making for home energy upgrades. Eligible customers purchasing solar and battery systems can access discounted finance offerings that are supported by the CEFC’s Household Energy Upgrades Fund. 

In line with the introduction of mandatory climate reporting in Australia, we also intend to review our data sources and methodology to identify opportunities to calculate our portfolio’s emissions intensity more accurately.

## **Dependencies and challenges** 

The reference scenarios that inform our goals assume the decarbonisation of Australia’s electricity grid, the uptake of distributed energy resources, construction of more energy efficient buildings, and building owners making operational improvements and/or retrofitting their buildings. State or Federal Government policies that support electrification and improved energy efficiency in new or existing buildings would also support achievement of our goals. 

In addition to the pace of grid decarbonisation, sector disruption in the form of rising construction costs and low construction labour availability may slow the transition and impact our ability to achieve our goals. 

## **Sector overview and outlook** 

As at 30 June 2024, lending to the Australian commercial property sector accounted for 6.9% of our in-scope drawn lending and 3.3% of our financed emissions. In Australia, commercial buildings use approximately 25% of overall electricity and contribute 10% of total carbon emissions.[ 2] Newer commercial properties tend to be more energy efficient and are expected to be better positioned for the transition, while older buildings need significant capital expenditure and upgrades to improve their energy efficiency. 

## **Our goals and portfolio** 

As at 30 June 2024, our 2030 goals cover 77%[ 3] of our in-scope drawn lending to the Australian commercial property sector and include the office, retail and industrial sub-sectors. We have set separate goals for these sub-sectors to recognise the different baseline emissions intensities for different types of buildings. This year we have changed our goals to reflect a material refresh of the Government’s Commercial Building Baseline Study (CBBS). Key changes in our goals for the retail and industrial sub-sectors are largely driven by reallocation of energy demand across building types in the 2024 CBBS as compared to the 2022 CBBS. Therefore our industrial sub-sector progress, which was above the reference scenario last year, is now below the reference scenario. It also means, we are unable to report year-on-year progress for these sub-sectors as the CBBS update does not provide comparative information. 

As at 30 June 2024, the emissions intensities of our office, retail and industrial portfolios were 58.1 kgCO2-e/m[2] , 48.6 kgCO2-e/m[2] and 30.8 kgCO2-e/m[2] , respectively. This is below the reference scenario for all portfolios, reflecting differences in our portfolio’s composition compared to the national average.

## **Sector overview and outlook** 

As at 30 June 2024, our Australian road goal covers 23% of our in-scope drawn lending to the transport sector and 67% of our in-scope drawn lending to the Australian motor vehicle finance sector. In 2023, Scope 1 emissions from the Australian transport sector accounted for approximately 21% of Australia’s total emissions, 2 with passenger and light commercial vehicles making up 60% of those emissions. Accelerating EV adoption and charging infrastructure investment is critical for reducing emissions. Policies like the New Vehicle Efficiency Standard aim to help consumers save money on fuel costs and provide more choice of new cars that are fuel-efficient, low or zero emissions, alongside environmental benefits. 

## **Our goal and portfolio** 

As at 30 June 2024, Australian road emissions intensity was 234 gCO2/vehicle km, a reduction of 11 gCO2/vehicle km compared to our 2023 baseline, and 15% below the reference scenario, reflecting increased lending to customers to purchase low-emissions vehicles.

## **Sector overview and outlook** 

As at 30 June 2024, the aviation goal covers 16% of our in-scope drawn lending to the transport sector and largely includes lending to international customers. In 2023, aviation accounted for over 2% of global energy-related emissions.2 Australia is highly dependent on the aviation sector to connect Australia to the world. ## **Our goal and portfolio**

As at 30 June 2024, aviation emissions intensity was 93 gCO2/revenue passenger km, a reduction of 10 gCO2/revenue passenger km compared to our 2023 baseline, and 6% below the reference scenario, reflecting new lending to airlines who operate newer aircraft with higher passenger load factors.

## **Heavy industry** 

**In-scope drawn lending as a proportion of Group TCE <0.1%** 

## **Sector overview and outlook** 

We define the heavy industry sector as the production of steel, alumina, aluminium and cement. As at 30 June 2024, lending to the heavy industry sector represented <0.1% of our in-scope drawn lending and 13.3% of our financed emissions. In 2023, Scope 1 emissions from the production of alumina, aluminium, iron, steel, lime, cement and other metals accounted for approximately 7% of Australia’s total emissions.2 Greenhouse gas emissions from heavy industry production generally occur from the high heat and chemical reactions required in manufacturing processes. These industries are also significant electricity consumers. 

Steel is essential for many other sectors, such as construction and machinery, and is expected to continue playing an important role in Australia’s energy transition, including the construction of wind turbines and solar farms. Cement is required for housing, buildings and infrastructure. Alumina is an intermediate product in the production of aluminium, which is used in technologies that are also critical for reducing emissions. Heavy industry is considered harder-to-abate due to its reliance on grid decarbonisation, process efficiencies and low-emissions technologies that, in many instances, are not yet commercially viable at scale in Australia. 

## **Our goals and portfolio** 

Our heavy industry portfolio is concentrated to a small number of companies. To protect our customers’ confidentiality, we have adopted a ‘traffic light’ disclosure of our portfolio against goals. This is to support cases where, for example, only one customer has drawn lending in the reporting period.
