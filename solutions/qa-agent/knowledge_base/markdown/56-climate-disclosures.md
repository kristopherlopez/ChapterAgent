CLIMATE DISCLOSURES 

## **Reviewing our operational emissions strategy and targets** 

This year we completed an evaluation of our operational emissions strategy as part of our five-year review cycle. The review considered our Scope 1 and 2, and Scope 3 reduction targets, boundaries and inventories, and incoming new mandatory climate reporting requirements. 

Given the significant progress in executing our reduction strategies for Scope 1 and 2, we are increasing our reduction target from 42% to 75% by 2030 against our 2020 baseline. We are also able to expand our Scope 1 and 2 target coverage from 2026 onwards as we now have better data for our other overseas offices, including India. 

We have also made several changes to improve how we measure, manage and represent Scope 3 operational emission sources, particularly those associated with our supply chain. Where we do not currently have activity data available, we are developing spend-based estimates. We have conservatively estimated our expanded footprint as an increase of approximately 600% from our reported 2024 operational emissions. As our current Scope 3 target accounts for only 13% of our expanded estimated emissions, the existing Scope 3 target is no longer meaningful in its present form. Accordingly, we will reassess this target and focus on more accurately quantifying our Scope 3 operational emissions in line with new mandatory climate reporting requirements in 2026. 

## **Supporting action outside our operations** 

In 2024, ASB pre-purchased carbon credits for 2025 sourced from GHG removal projects under the Permanent Forest Sink Initiative (PFSI). In 2024, Toitū Envirocare announced its commitment to align their practices with international standards. As a result, they will be moving towards no longer accepting carbon credits issued under the New Zealand PFSI for offsetting purposes. Toitū Envirocare honours the use of pre-purchased, ‘reserved’ New Zealand credits. As part of the transition phase, ASB will apply 2024 pre-purchased credits sourced from New Zealand-based PFSI compliant projects to maintain its Toitū Envirocare Net Carbon Zero certification. 

We do not use carbon credits to meet our operational emissions targets. However, we continue to support climate action beyond our value chain, such as purchasing carbon credits and investing to help reduce our travel emissions. 

This year CBA signed several multi-year agreements supporting improved social, cultural and environmental impacts through projects within the ACCU scheme. Our portfolio focuses on projects supporting Australia’s Indigenous communities and aligns with our Reconciliation Action Plan. This year CBA supported Indigenous savanna burning projects which also have benefits for nature. Selected projects went through a due diligence assessment, to assess the integrity and credibility of the carbon credits generated. This due diligence assessment was based on the Integrity Council for the Voluntary Carbon Market’s Core Carbon Principles and transparency changes to the ACCU scheme. 

Given air travel emissions are particularly hard to reduce, CBA has been part of the Qantas SAF coalition for the past two years. The coalition aims to support the scaling of the SAF industry to reduce aviation emissions. During the year we increased our level of participation to become a ‘partner’, which contributes to the airline’s incremental cost of purchasing SAF. The use of SAF purchased through CBA’s partnership would be equivalent to avoiding 1,000 tCO₂-e of CBA’s air travel emissions. 

We continue to monitor developments related to the ACCU scheme and note the review Climate Active is currently undertaking. We will reassess our position as more information becomes available. 

## **Actions to help us reduce our supply chain emissions** 

Air travel emissions represent 70% of the emissions in our current Scope 3 operational emissions target. Our efforts to date have been directed towards mitigating our own air travel impacts and supporting lower-carbon alternative fuels. Our Australian business monitors business travel and tracks actual emissions against internal carbon budgets to balance the demand for business critical travel while focusing on meeting our Scope 3 target. 

We also continue to take steps to reduce the impact of our office and branch fit-outs by making them more resource efficient. Our refurbishment projects incorporate sustainable design principles, and we look to reuse and repurpose existing building materials and furniture, wherever possible. 

Our three-year ATM refresh project, covering approximately 1,700 ATMs, is implementing a modular construction system using lower-carbon, natural and renewable materials. A circular design approach is being taken, facilitating efficient installation and easy disassembly for repairs and reuse. In addition, our latest container branch, designed to support communities after natural disasters, considers sustainable design principles. The container itself has been reused, and approximately 70% of its interior is fitted with lower-carbon and circular materials, such as wall linings made from upcycled packaging, recycled plastic acoustic panelling and furniture, as well as lower-carbon carpet and joinery panels. 

We also measure the impact of our offices via major projects aiming to achieve lower embodied carbon compared to previous comparable buildings in our portfolio. This year we completed the Bankwest Place refurbishment in Perth, achieving a 42% reduction in embodied carbon. This is compared to the estimated emissions for a typical fitout with conventional material selections. 

## **Understanding the impact of climate and nature issues on Australian agriculture** 

Australian agriculture is exposed to both climate- and nature-related risks and opportunities given the emissions created, carbon sequestered into land, and the impacts and dependencies to produce food and fibre for our economy. 

## **Continuing to support Australian agriculture** 

The Australian agriculture sector is essential for our economy and food security, and is central to regional communities and the financial health of their economies. The sector accounts for 18% of Australia’s net emissions, or 16% excluding land-use, land-use change and forestry. 1 Landowners face risks including acute weather events, a changing climate and loss of ecosystem services.

The agriculture sector faces specific challenges in goal and target setting, including measuring emissions, multiple gas sources, different agricultural activities, and the way emissions are generated and stored through natural systems. We continued to explore the possibility of setting financed emissions goals and targets for the Australian agriculture sector, which were deferred in 2024 due to data and methodology challenges.
